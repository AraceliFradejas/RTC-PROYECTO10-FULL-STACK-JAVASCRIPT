At one of our workshops, a team arrived with a clear proposal: we want artificial intelligence to tell us which requests to handle first. The demonstration was fast, tidy and quite convincing.

Before trying it, I asked a question: what does first mean? The most urgent? The easiest? The one that has been waiting longest? Within minutes, we had three different criteria. The tool could not resolve a disagreement we had not yet acknowledged.

We worked with fictional cases and agreed on the decision we wanted to support. Then we examined the information we needed and what was missing. A short request is not always simple. Someone who writes more does not always need more help.

Only then did we compare the system's suggestion with a review by someone who understood the service. When they disagreed, we did not automatically choose either answer. We looked for the reason behind the difference.

That exercise taught us more than a screen full of results. It helped us uncover assumptions, refine our criteria and decide when a person needed to review a case.

Before your next experiment with artificial intelligence, write down three things: which decision you want to improve, what information would be sufficient, and how you would recognise a wrong result. If you cannot explain those yet, start there.

The speed of an answer can impress us. Its value lies in explaining why it deserves our trust, when it does not, and who takes responsibility for acting on it.
